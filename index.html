01 Harold's note

It's not about going without. It's about paying attention.

I have never believed that a good meal needs to announce how much it cost. Some of the best food I know starts with an onion in a pan, a potato on a cutting board, a pot of beans, a chicken bone saved for stock, or a loaf of bread that has gone a little dry.

The older I get, the less impressed I am by waste. I want the bones simmered, the extra rice cooled and saved, the last pieces of chicken turned into another supper, and the cabbage cooked until somebody at the table asks what smells so good.

I still want crisp edges, glossy gravy, soft bread, something sweet with tea, and a Friday-night table that feels generous. The trick is learning where money improves a meal and where attention does the job better.

— Grandpa Harold

02 The idea

Make the meal taste richer than the receipt.

Frugal cooking doesn't mean buying the cheapest food. It means getting more meals, more flavor and less waste out of what you already buy. Every chapter asks one question: can an ordinary ingredient do more than one job?

  1. i

    Cheaper foundations

    Beans, grains, potatoes, onions, cabbage, bread and eggs carry more of the plate.

  2. ii

    Technique before price

    Browning, resting, seasoning and slow cooking make modest ingredients taste deliberate.

  3. iii

    Second meals

    Roasts, rice, bread and vegetables are cooked with the next use already planned.

  4. iv

    Fewer convenience foods

    Stock, crumbs, sauces and simple doughs get made at home when the savings are worth the time.

  5. v

    Less waste

    A bargain is only a bargain when the food actually gets eaten.

05 A page from the book

Recipe 2: Slow-Browned Onions.

Every recipe gets three pages: the cost line and ingredients, the method, and Harold's notebook on saving more, using it twice and making it ahead.

PAREVE · Makes about 3 cups · Prep 10 min · Cook 45–60 min

At home$2–$4
Prepared equivalent$8–$14
Potential difference$4–$12

A pan of onions cooked slowly gives beans, eggs, potatoes, noodles and sandwiches the deep flavor people often mistake for expensive ingredients.

Why this saves: Time is the expensive ingredient here, not groceries. One pan saves you from buying sauces and flavor packets all week.

Ingredients

  • 4 large yellow onions, thinly sliced
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • ½ teaspoon salt
  • 2–4 tablespoons water, as needed
  • Black pepper, optional

Method: cook it without rushing it.

  1. Heat the oil in a wide heavy skillet over medium-low heat.
  2. Add the onions and salt. Stir until every slice is lightly coated.
  3. Cook slowly, stirring every few minutes. When brown bits form, add a spoonful of water and scrape them back into the onions.
  4. Keep going until the onions are soft, deep golden and cooked down to about a third of their original volume.
  5. Cool, then refrigerate in a covered container.

Harold's notebook

Use it twice. Fold into scrambled eggs, kasha, mashed potatoes, soup or leftover chicken. A tablespoon can change an entire bowl.

Make ahead. Keeps about 4 days in the fridge. Freeze in tablespoon-size portions to keep it longer.

06 About the cook

Harold Rosenberg. A lifetime at the family stove.

Harold never went to cooking school. He learned the way most people in his family did: standing next to his mother and grandmother on Friday afternoons, being handed an onion and told to slice it thinner.

He learned from Shabbat preparation and holiday cooking, from recipe cards with half the measurements missing, from pantry habits, from leftovers that turned into something new, from preserving what was in season, and from buying ingredients nobody else thought were worth much. And he learned from many years of his own mistakes, which he's happy to tell you about so you don't make them.

His cooking is Ashkenazi and Eastern European home food, the kind made in family kitchens rather than restaurants. He explains it the way a grandfather does across the kitchen table: calmly, practically and with the occasional joke. He's not reading off a recipe card. He's telling you what actually matters.

"Frugality is not a purity test. It is the long habit of noticing what you already have, using it well, and spending with intention."

Harold's rules, from the margins

  • Save scraps before they become waste.
  • Build flavor once, then use it in several meals.
  • Don't confuse expensive with good.
  • Make food people want to eat. Saving money on a meal nobody enjoys is not a victory.

08 Questions

Before you set the table.

Is this a kosher cookbook?

The book draws mostly on Ashkenazi and Eastern European Jewish home cooking. Every recipe is labeled MEAT, DAIRY, PAREVE or FISH to help you plan, but those labels are not rabbinic certification. If you keep kosher, check ingredients, equipment and preparation against the standards you follow. Customs vary from household to household, and Harold respects that.

Do I need to be an experienced cook?

No. Chapter one covers the five foundations: stock from bones, slow-browned onions, a breadcrumb jar, cook-once beans, and schmaltz. Everything after builds on those. Cook a method once as written, and use the variations after you know the texture and timing.

Are the dollar figures guaranteed savings?

No. They're planning ranges in U.S. dollars, not quotes from a particular store or restaurant. Prices change with location, season, package size, taxes, tips and delivery fees. Harold's advice is to make a dish once and write down what you actually spent. Your household's number is more useful than anybody's estimate.

I don't live in the U.S. Will the measurements work?

Yes. The book includes a conversion table for cups, spoons, ounces and pounds, and every oven temperature is given in both Fahrenheit and Celsius.

What format is it?

A 260-page PDF. It opens on any phone, tablet or computer, and prints cleanly on ordinary paper if you'd like a copy with stains on it.

Does it cover Shabbat and the holidays?

Yes. There's a full chapter on Shabbat on a budget (roast chicken, cholent, challah, tzimmes) and another on holidays, with Rosh Hashanah honey chicken, Passover potato kugel, a Hanukkah latke tray, hamantaschen and Sukkot stuffed squash.

Cook through it slowly. Mark what your family likes.
